Spring 2024 · Electric Circuits I

Fourth-order op-amp RLC network analysis

A complete analytical study of a fourth-order op-amp RLC network — closed-form responses, natural frequencies, a state-space model and driving-point impedance — with every result verified against simulation.

LTspicestate-space modellingtransient analysisphasor analysiscircuit theory

What was analysed

The circuit combines an op-amp and a dependent source with three capacitors and an inductor — four energy storage elements, so the response cannot be read off by inspection. The network was attacked from several independent directions so that each result could be checked against the others.

Time domain

Starting from the zero-input response, node equations at each stage produced first-order differential equations solved outward from the input toward the output. The natural frequencies fell out of those solutions as a set of decaying exponentials with distinct time constants. From there the step response and the impulse response were derived, the impulse response obtained by differentiating the step response and tracking the delta terms carefully through each stage.

State-space model

Taking the three capacitor voltages and the inductor current as state variables produced a four-variable state-space model. Each row came from applying KCL or KVL at the element in question, giving a compact matrix description of the whole circuit that agreed with the time-domain solutions.

Frequency domain and power

Under sinusoidal steady-state conditions the same network was re-analysed with phasors and complex impedances. The driving-point impedance and admittance seen from the source terminals were derived using a test-source method, and from those the power delivered and the efficiency were computed.

Verification

Every analytical result was checked against LTspice: transient simulations for the zero-input, step and impulse responses, and a sinusoidal source for the steady-state and power calculations. Where the simulated waveform disagreed with the hand derivation, the derivation was the thing that turned out to be wrong.

What I took from it

This was the project where analysis stopped being an exercise in algebra. Deriving the same circuit four different ways and having the answers agree is a much stronger form of understanding than getting one of them right.
