Digital Systems II, autumn 2025

Water level and temperature control system

Bare-metal firmware for an STM32F103C8T6 that regulates both the water level of a 50 cm tank and the water temperature, without using any third-party sensor libraries.

STM32F103C8T6ARM Cortex-M3bare-metal CTIM2 input captureEXTIADCPWMHC-SR04LM35RTCUART

The problem

The project required a controller to regulate two physical variables simultaneously: the water level in a tank and its temperature. Both control loops had to run continuously while the user changed setpoints through a keypad, without either loop being stalled by the display or real-time clock.

Measuring the tank without a driver

An HC-SR04 ultrasonic sensor mounted above the tank measures the distance to the water surface, which is used to determine the water level.

Because the sensor had to be interfaced without a third-party library, the echo pulse is timed directly using TIM2 input capture and interrupt handling. The rising and falling edges are captured in hardware rather than polled in software, allowing the rest of the system to continue running while the measurement is taken.

Two control loops

The level loop drives the inlet pump using a 100 Hz PWM signal, with the duty cycle scaled according to the level error: full power at maximum error and the pump fully off at zero or negative error.

The temperature loop follows the same control structure: an LM35 is sampled through the ADC, and a heating element is driven by PWM toward a 60 °C setpoint.

Interface

A 16×4 LCD displays the current water level, active setpoint, and water temperature, with the RTC time shown on the bottom line of each screen. A 4×4 keypad and EXTI-driven navigation buttons open a menu for entering a new setpoint or setting the clock. Entered values are range-validated and rejected on screen if they fall outside the allowed range; successful changes are confirmed over UART; and if the temperature drops below its threshold, the display clears and blinks a warning.

What I took from it

The real difficulty was not any single feature, but coordinating them on a single Cortex-M3. Interrupt-driven sensing, two PWM outputs, a scanned keypad, a character display, and a real-time clock all had to operate continuously without one function blocking the others.
